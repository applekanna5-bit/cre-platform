An occupied site can contain a house the community owner does not own. That is the first fact to verify when reading a manufactured housing community's rent roll. In a land-lease community, a resident can own the home and rent the developed site beneath it. An operator renting out both homes and sites earns a different stream of income and takes on different expenses.

## Read the site inventory beside the home inventory

A **site**, often called a **pad**, is the developed space where a home can be installed. Record the total sites, the sites that can be leased now, those occupied by resident-owned or park-owned homes, and those down because of infrastructure or another restriction. A vacant home can occupy a site. A leased site can also have an obligation payable by someone other than the household currently living there. Reconcile physical status, lease status, billing and collections separately.

A mixed community may have borrower-owned homes, homes owned by an affiliate, and resident-owned homes. The affiliate can collect the home rent even if the community borrower collects the site rent. Other communities are resident-owned through a cooperative that holds the land. Read the ownership and lease documents before modeling what the borrowing entity can collect. When a resident sells a home in place, check whether the buyer takes a new site lease or needs consent. The home sale and the site's rental income are separate transactions.

## Find the water bill and follow it through the ledger

Read the lot lease and community rules for rent, included services, utility billing, responsibilities, renewal and transfer terms. Ask who owns each utility line and meter. A public utility can bill residents directly, or the operator can receive a master bill and recover part of the cost. Where the operator bills residents, confirm what the leases and applicable rules allow and reconcile billed amounts with cash collected. Water leaks, common-area use and uncollectible bills can leave the operator with a recurring shortfall.

The property inspection needs to cover roads, drainage, water distribution and wastewater, in addition to visible buildings. A community served by municipal water and sewer can still own the lines within the property. At a private well or wastewater system, identify the owner and operator, inspect maintenance and compliance records, obtain capacity and condition evidence, and check what permits or upgrades the actual system requires. Confirm access and utility easements where the community depends on adjoining land. A failed line may take sites out of service and require a capital outlay at the same time.

## Follow the loan collateral through a fictional rent roll

A fictional property has 100 developed sites. Resident-owned homes occupy 80 sites, park-owned rentals occupy 12, four usable sites are vacant, and four sites are down. Overall physical site occupancy is 92%. Among the 96 usable sites, occupancy is about 95.8%. Report both denominators and keep the four down sites in the capital review. Returning them to service needs a repair budget and a realistic lease-up plan.

Assume each occupied site contributes $600 in scheduled monthly site rent, including the site portion of bundled park-owned home leases. The 12 park-owned homes add $450 per month each for the home component. On a flat twelve-month schedule, site rent is $662,400 and incremental home rent is $64,800. The allocation is a fictional analytical assumption. Bound it with actual leases where rent is bundled.

Assume annual utility reimbursements of $48,000, after any losses on those reimbursements, against $66,000 of utility expense. Deduct $18,000 of rental losses from the occupied rent schedule. This schedule gives no rent to the eight vacant or down sites, so there is no further vacancy deduction for them. Revenue is $757,200 after the stated losses. Operating costs are $310,000, including the full utility expense and $18,000 of direct home expenses. Pre-reserve NOI is $447,200.

Suppose the analyst tests site operations without relying on home rent. Remove the $64,800 gross home component, add back $4,800 of losses allocated solely to it, and remove $18,000 of direct home expenses. On the stated assumptions, the result is $405,200 of pre-reserve site-only NOI. It still carries all shared expenses and the $18,000 utility shortfall. A separate $35,000 annual reserve allowance gives $370,200 before debt service. That allowance represents recurring provision in the scenario. A near-term utility replacement still needs its own cost estimate and funding plan.

The site-only view is a sensitivity, not a program rule or automatic appraisal adjustment. The lender and appraiser need to agree on the property rights and income being valued. Support rent assumptions with comparable sites and lease terms rather than apartment rents. When using a capitalization rate, check whether its comparable sales included homes or other assets that this mortgage would exclude. Carry the same income and collateral assumptions into the debt service and refinance analysis. If the home income stays in the analysis, check the related costs and capital needs as carefully as the revenue.

## Check title and program treatment before credit approval

A home can be titled as personal property or treated as real property under applicable law. A loan secured by a resident's home is distinct from a mortgage on the community. For borrower-owned or affiliate-owned homes, reconcile titles, ownership, any liens and the proposed collateral with counsel. The home being physically present on the site does not establish that the community lender has a lien on it.
