A warehouse and a hotel can sell for the same price and support very different loans. The warehouse may collect most of its income from one tenant on a long lease. The hotel has to sell rooms again tomorrow. Before looking at either property's valuation, ask who pays, what they pay for, and how quickly the income could change.

This guide focuses on U.S. commercial real estate used for business or investment, particularly properties that earn income. An apartment community belongs in that discussion even though the people who live there use it as housing. An owner-occupied building is also commercial real estate, but repayment may depend more on the owner's operating business than on rent paid by independent tenants. Land and projects under development may have little or no current income.

## Start with how the property earns money

In **multifamily**, residents pay for the use of units. Unit mix, turnover, occupancy and concessions help explain the rent that actually comes in. Affordable housing restrictions can change what the owner may charge or who may occupy units. In a manufactured housing community, the operator may collect site rent while a resident owns the home. The income being analyzed depends on what the property owner actually owns and leases.

In **retail**, look at who draws customers to the center and which leases expire together. A lease may include reimbursement of certain property costs or rent tied to sales. In **office**, a lease that expires next year can bring downtime, leasing commissions and tenant improvements even if the tenant pays on time today. **Industrial** can mean a distribution building, small-bay space or a specialized facility. A highly specialized layout may take longer to relet when a tenant leaves.

**Hospitality** earns room revenue and may earn money from food, events and other services. Room rates and occupancy can move quickly, and running the property is part of earning the income. A **mixed-use** building can combine several of these models. Separate them when their income, expenses or lease risks behave differently.

## The property, its owner and its capital

The deed may name a limited liability company while the **sponsor** is the person or group directing the investment. Equity can come from the sponsor, other private investors, a fund or a real estate investment trust. An investor may own an interest in the entity rather than a direct slice of the property. Ask who makes decisions, who is putting in cash, and whether anyone has committed additional support.

**Equity** absorbs changes in the residual value and receives what is left after expenses, debt payments and other obligations under the applicable agreements. **Debt** supplies capital in exchange for contractual repayment. A mortgage or deed of trust gives the lender a security interest in the real estate under the applicable documents and law. A guarantee, if present, is a separate promise whose scope must be read on its own terms.

## Follow the income before estimating value

Suppose a fictional neighborhood retail property collects $520,000 in annual rent and $80,000 in expense reimbursements. It pays $240,000 in annual property operating costs, including the costs being reimbursed. On this simplified basis, annual **net operating income (NOI)** is $360,000. Counting the reimbursement while omitting its related cost would overstate the result.

The example measures income before debt service and assumes no capital costs or reserve deductions in this NOI figure. Actual analyses must say how they treat major replacements and reserves. If annual debt service is $250,000, the arithmetic difference is $110,000 before other cash needs. That residual is neither a promise of distributions nor a substitute for a capital budget.
