Two apartment properties can have the same contract-to-market rent gap and very different income prospects.

At the first, recent leases support the estimated market rents, expirations are distributed throughout the year, and residents have been renewing at higher effective rents without meaningful occupancy disruption. At the second, market-rent estimates rely largely on asking rents, many leases expire within one quarter, and achieving higher face rents requires concessions.

Both properties might show $130,000 of annualized rent potential. They should not necessarily receive the same underwriting treatment.

The arithmetic measures the difference between current contractual rents and estimated market rents. It cannot establish whether the property can achieve those rents, when existing leases can change, or what revenue will remain after concessions and vacancy.

**The gap is a measurement. The opportunity is an underwriting conclusion.**

## What contract rent actually represents

Contract rent is the rent established by an existing lease, subject to its provisions and amendments. For an occupied apartment, it describes a contractual payment obligation; it does not guarantee collection.

A rent roll captures contractual arrangements at a point in time. A trailing operating statement describes historical property performance over a period, subject to the accounting conventions used. Those are related but different views.

Suppose an apartment has a contractual monthly rent of $1,400. Multiplying by twelve produces $16,800 of annualized contractual rent, not necessarily $16,800 of revenue recognized or collected over the next twelve months. The lease might expire, a concession might apply, or the resident could become delinquent. A vacancy following turnover would change the result again.

Contract rent is an essential input, not a substitute for a revenue forecast.

## Market rent must be supported, not merely entered

Market rent estimates what a comparable unit could command under prevailing market conditions and relevant lease terms. It differs from asking rent, the amount advertised or requested by an owner. An executed lease provides evidence of an agreed rate; effective rent reflects the economic impact of relevant concessions over a defined period.

A renovated apartment may command a premium over an otherwise similar unrenovated unit. A high asking rent may require a concession. One successful lease may reflect unusual timing, negotiation, or unit characteristics rather than a property-wide trend.

To assess the market-rent column, investigate whether the observations are executed leases or advertisements; whether units are comparable in size, condition, amenities, and location; whether concessions alter effective economics; and whether the sample is recent and representative. Subject-property leasing should be reconciled with outside comparable evidence rather than assumed to prove it.

<InstitutionalExample sourceId="freddie-appraisal-checklist-2026">Freddie Mac's February 2026 Multifamily Underwriting Appraisal Checklist requests support for concluded market rents by unit type using subject-property leases commencing within 90 days of the effective date of value. This appraisal checklist expresses an institution-specific evidentiary expectation, not a universal underwriting or rent-setting rule.</InstitutionalExample>

One strong lease is evidence. A consistent pattern of comparable leasing provides stronger support.

## Face rent and effective economics

Two hypothetical twelve-month leases show why higher face rent need not produce more rent after concessions. Lease A charges $1,400 monthly without a concession: $16,800 over twelve months. Lease B charges $1,450 but offers one month free: $15,950 after that concession, or $1,329.17 in average monthly effective rent.

<Calculation inputs={[{label: "Lease B face rent", value: "$1,450/month"}, {label: "Lease term", value: "12 months"}, {label: "Concession", value: "One month free at $1,450"}]} formula="($1,450 × 12 − $1,450) ÷ 12" result="$1,329.17/month average effective rent" interpretation="Hypothetical initial-term effective-rent illustration, not a lender-prescribed concession methodology or a Harbor View lease." />

Lease B may still be a sensible business decision in some circumstances. But comparing face rents without the concession would misstate the initial-term economics.

<Warning>A higher advertised or signed face rent should not be treated as a higher lease-term effective rent without considering concessions over the relevant lease period. In this illustration, lease-term effective rent spreads contractual rent less concessions over the lease term. Vacancy and downtime between leases affect the broader revenue forecast separately. Do not count the same concession or vacancy effect twice in a revenue model.</Warning>

## A practical framework for underwriting the gap

The CRE Platform uses **Compare → Validate → Time → Adjust → Underwrite** as an original educational framework, not an agency rule.

**Compare:** Measure the contract-to-market difference using consistent units, periods, and rent definitions.

**Validate:** Test market assumptions against representative executed leases and comparable evidence.

**Time:** Consider expiration dates, renewal opportunities, and potential turnover.

**Adjust:** Account for concessions, vacancy, collection exposure, and costs without double-counting.

**Underwrite:** Make a conclusion that can be explained using the available evidence and the relevant institutional methodology.

The framework does not prescribe a capture percentage. It prevents a market-rent column from becoming an automatic revenue forecast.

## The gap is a starting point

A positive gap may identify revenue opportunity; a negative gap may indicate rollover exposure. A gap concentrated in a few apartments has a different risk profile from the same total spread across a property. A gap supported by representative effective leases is more credible than one based on optimistic asking rents.

Harbor View's $131,280 annualized difference matters because it identifies something worth investigating. It does not establish $131,280 of additional collectible revenue, higher NOI, or greater loan proceeds.

**The rent gap tells us what might change. Underwriting determines what we can reasonably rely on.**
