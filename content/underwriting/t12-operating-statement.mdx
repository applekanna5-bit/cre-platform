Harbor View Apartments reports **$3.180 million of effective gross income**, **$965,000 of operating expenses**, and **$2.215 million of NOI** for the trailing twelve months.

The math works.

**The calculation is only the first pass.**

Rental income increased during the year. Concessions also increased. Insurance became noticeably more expensive toward the end of the period. Repairs jumped in one month. Other income spiked in August and immediately returned to its previous level.

None of those observations changes the property's reported T-12 NOI.

They change what the analyst needs to investigate.

> **The annual column tells you what the period adds up to. The monthly columns help explain how the property got there.**

That is the difference between reading an operating statement and underwriting one.

## What a T-12 actually tells you

A T-12, or trailing twelve-month operating statement, shows a property's operating results over the most recent twelve months covered by the statement.

It is a rolling historical period. It does not have to correspond to a calendar year.

Harbor View's statement, for example, covers **October 2025 through September 2026**.

Depending on the owner's accounting system and reporting format, a T-12 may contain rental income, vacancy, concessions, bad debt or collection loss, other income, operating expenses and NOI.

The presentation is not standardized across every borrower or property.

That matters more than it may initially appear.

Two operating statements can use different labels for economically similar items. Conversely, two lines carrying similar labels may not necessarily represent exactly the same thing.

Harbor View, for example, uses **gross rental income** as a fictional operating-statement line item. That should not automatically be treated as identical to a lender-defined **Gross Potential Rent (GPR)** calculation.

Before analyzing a number, understand what the number represents.

A T-12 is therefore valuable historical evidence. It is not automatically the underwriting conclusion.

And historical does not mean irrelevant.

The analyst's job is to determine how historical performance informs the period being underwritten.

## Start vertically

The first pass through Harbor View is straightforward.

Read down the statement.

The revenue reconciliation is:

**$3.126M − $60K − $36K − $42K = $2.988M of net rental income**

Then:

**$2.988M + $192K = $3.180M of effective gross income**

And:

**$3.180M − $965K = $2.215M of NOI**

Now we know what produced the reported result. We still don't know whether every component is representative.

It also forces an important distinction.

Physical vacancy and economic losses are related, but they are not identical.

A vacant unit can reduce rental revenue because nobody occupies it. An occupied unit can still create economic loss through concessions or uncollected rent.

If all three are buried under the idea of "vacancy," useful information disappears.

So the vertical read answers:

**What makes up the result?**

Now turn the analysis ninety degrees.

## Read the T-12 horizontally

Harbor View's gross rental income progresses like this:

**$255K → $256K → $257K → … → $265K → $266K**

At first glance, that's encouraging.

Gross rental income increased throughout the year.

Now look at concessions.

They begin around **$2,000 per month** and finish at:

**$4K → $5K → $7K**

Bad debt and collection loss also remain part of the picture.

The result is that net rental income moves from **$245K in October to $251K in September** and sits around $250K–$251K during much of the later period.

So this statement:

**"Rental income increased."**

is mathematically true.

But it is incomplete.

Some of the gross improvement is being absorbed elsewhere in the revenue structure.

That is why we don't read a T-12 only vertically.

We read it horizontally too.

**Vertical:** What makes up the result?

**Horizontal:** How did those components behave through time?

<PractitionerNote>This vertical/horizontal distinction is a CRE Platform analytical framework, not an agency-prescribed methodology.</PractitionerNote>

The distinction is simple, but it changes how you look at almost every operating statement.

## Now the expense story starts to appear

Look at five Harbor View expense categories:

Three lines immediately deserve attention.

But not necessarily the same treatment.

### Insurance

Insurance moves higher late in the period.

The T-12 total is **$120,000**, while annualizing the most recent three months produces **$184,000**.

That does **not** mean:

> Underwrite insurance at $184,000.

It means:

**Something changed. Find out what.**

Was there a renewal?

Did the premium increase?

Did coverage change?

Is there a current policy or premium statement that better represents the cost expected during the underwriting period?

The T-12 gives us the signal.

Supporting evidence gives us the explanation.

### Payroll

Payroll behaves differently.

Instead of one dramatic month, the expense gradually increases.

That produces:

**T-12:** $210K  
**T-6 annualized:** $222K  
**T-3 annualized:** $232K

The pattern raises a different set of questions.

Was staffing added?

Did wages increase?

Was a previously vacant position filled?

Did ownership change the operating model?

Is the latest staffing structure expected to continue?

Again, there is no automatic adjustment hiding inside the T-3 number.

There is an underwriting question.

### Repairs and maintenance

Repairs show another pattern entirely.

Most months are around **$10,000**.

Then July reports:

**$35,000.**

That single month pushes the annualized T-3 repairs figure to **$220,000**, compared with **$145,000** for the full T-12.

Two shortcuts would both be dangerous.

The first is:

> "Recent repairs are higher. Use $220,000."

The second is:

> "July is unusual. Remove it."

Neither tells us what actually happened.

Maybe July included ordinary recurring work that happened to be concentrated in one month.

Maybe there was a discrete repair.

Maybe the property was catching up on deferred work.

Maybe something was classified differently.

We don't know yet.

And **"we don't know yet" is a perfectly valid underwriting conclusion when the evidence is incomplete.**

The next step is investigation, not assumption.

## Other income deserves the same discipline

Harbor View reports **$192,000 of other income** during the T-12.

For eleven months, the property reports:

**$15,000 per month.**

In August:

**$27,000.**

There is an obvious $12,000 difference.

But spotting it doesn't tell us what to do with it.

It could represent recurring operating income recognized unevenly, a reimbursement, an accounting classification or a genuinely nonrecurring item.

So don't start with:

> "Remove $12,000."

Start with:

> **"What is the $12,000?"**

The pattern tells us to investigate.

The source and nature of the income determine the underwriting treatment.

This distinction matters because underwriting can become dangerously subjective when every unusual historical number is treated as permission to "normalize" the statement.

## A red flag is a question, not automatically an adjustment

This principle applies throughout the T-12.

An analyst should notice sudden movements without a clear explanation; increasing concessions despite rising gross rent; unusual collection losses; revenue concentrated in one or two months; insurance or payroll stepping higher; large repair months; expenses disappearing unexpectedly; unusually low expenses without support; unexplained miscellaneous categories; and patterns that conflict with other property information.

Notice that not every red flag is negative.

An unexpectedly low expense can deserve just as much scrutiny as an unexpectedly high one.

An unexplained revenue spike can deserve just as much attention as a revenue decline.

The point isn't to hunt for bad news.

The point is to understand what is economically happening at the property.

> **A red flag is a question, not automatically an adjustment.**

That discipline matters later, when historical results begin turning into underwriting assumptions.

## Don't make the T-12 work alone

A T-12 becomes substantially more useful when it is reconciled with other evidence.

Suppose Harbor View's current rent roll shows rents materially above the average implied by the earlier months of the T-12.

That could help explain the upward rental-income trend.

But the rent roll and T-12 should not be expected to match line for line.

They describe the property differently.

The **T-12 is a historical flow of operating activity across a period.**

The **rent roll is generally a point-in-time view of units, tenants or leases and their current contractual information.**

That difference is fundamental.

The question isn't:

**"Why don't these documents match?"**

It is:

**"Does the current rent roll explain, confirm or challenge the trajectory visible in the historical operating statement?"**

The same approach applies to other information.

Prior operating statements can show whether this year's pattern is unusual.

The budget can show management's expectations—but those assumptions still need to be understood and supported.

Collection or receivable information can help explain whether reported rental activity is translating into collections.

Current insurance documentation can help explain a late-period expense increase.

Tax information can tell us whether the historical expense is relevant to the period being underwritten.

Contracts, invoices and general-ledger detail can help explain unusual expenses.

None of these documents needs to be forced into agreement.

> **Reconciliation means understanding why the documents differ—not making them agree.**

## Four questions for almost every T-12 line

We can now reduce the process to a practical framework.

Take Harbor View insurance.

**Total**

What did the property report?

**$120,000.**

**Trend**

How did the expense behave?

It increased materially during the later months.

**Reconciliation**

What does current insurance documentation tell us about that increase?

**Underwriting Question**

What amount best represents the insurance cost for the underwriting period under the applicable methodology?

Now use the same framework for repairs.

**Total**

$145,000.

**Trend**

Most months are approximately $10,000, with a $35,000 July.

**Reconciliation**

What caused July's expense?

**Underwriting Question**

Does that historical activity represent normal recurring operations, or does the evidence support a different interpretation?

Same framework.

Different evidence.

Potentially different conclusion.

That is exactly how it should work.

## What the T-12 cannot answer

A good T-12 contains a great deal of information.

It still doesn't tell us everything.

By itself, it may not establish current contractual rents, lease expirations, future tenant rollover, market rents, future property-tax changes, the current insurance premium, future capital requirements, the sustainability of an unusual income item or the lender-specific methodology that will ultimately govern underwriting.

That's why the T-12 should neither be dismissed as "just historical" nor accepted unquestioningly because it contains twelve months of actual numbers.

It is evidence.

And underwriting is the process of deciding what that evidence means.

## Back to Harbor View

We started with one number:

**$2.215 million of T-12 NOI.**

We still have exactly the same number.

But we understand it differently now.

Gross rental income improved.

Increasing concessions absorbed part of that improvement.

Net rental income remained modestly stronger on a recent annualized basis.

Other income contains an August spike that needs explanation.

Insurance moved materially higher.

Payroll increased gradually.

Repairs contain one conspicuous month.

Taxes and utilities show no obvious monthly anomaly within this statement.

And recent NOI is weaker even though recent NRI is slightly stronger.

That last point is particularly important.

It tells us not to reduce the property's story to:

**"NOI is declining."**

The statement is telling us something more specific:

**Recent expense behavior is putting pressure on NOI while rental performance remains comparatively resilient.**

Now we have something useful to investigate.

We do **not** yet have underwritten NOI.

That comes later.

## From reported performance to underwriting

A T-12 answers an important question:

**What happened?**

Good underwriting keeps going.

**When did it happen?**

**Why did it happen?**

**Does other evidence support the explanation?**

**And what does that mean for the period we're underwriting?**

That's why the annual NOI subtotal is the beginning of the analysis—not the end.

> **Don't ask only what the T-12 totals. Ask how the property got there.**

The natural next document is the **rent roll**.

Harbor View's T-12 has shown us how rental operations behaved historically. The rent roll will help us understand what is happening at the unit and lease level now.

Then we can start reconciling the two.

And eventually, that evidence feeds into the larger question:

**What operating performance can the underwriter actually support?**
