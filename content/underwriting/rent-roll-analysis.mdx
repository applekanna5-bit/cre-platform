Harbor View Apartments is **95% occupied**.

Its September 30 rent roll shows 171 occupied units, seven vacant units, two non-revenue units, and **$251,910 of monthly occupied contractual rent**.

At first glance, the property looks straightforward.

Occupancy is high. Recent leases are generally stronger than older leases. Several units are leasing at or near the property's stated market rents.

But four recent leases in our review sample carry concessions. Fifty-five occupied leases expire between April and June 2027. Some older residents remain well below the property's stated market rents. And the $251,910 shown on the rent roll is not the same thing as the rental income reported on Harbor View's T-12.

None of those observations tells us what rental revenue should be underwritten.

They tell us what to investigate.

> **The rows contain the data. The pattern contains the underwriting question.**

That is where rent-roll analysis begins.

## What a rent roll actually tells you

A rent roll is a point-in-time view of a property's leasing structure.

For a multifamily property, it commonly contains information such as unit number, unit type, occupancy status, contractual rent, lease commencement and expiration dates, and sometimes market rent, concessions or other unit-level information.

The exact format varies.

That matters because the analyst needs to understand what the source document means before calculating ratios from it.

Harbor View's September 30 rent roll contains:

<FinancialTable tableId="harbor-view-rent-roll-inventory" />

On a total-unit basis:

**171 ÷ 180 = 95.0% occupied**

The calculation is simple.

The interpretation is not.

Before relying on that percentage, the analyst should understand what happened to the other nine units.

How long have the seven vacant units been vacant? Are any pre-leased? Why are two units classified as non-revenue? Are they employee units, models, down units or something else?

A summary ratio describes the property.

The underlying rows help explain it.

## Physical occupancy is not economic vacancy

Harbor View's 95% calculation tells us that 171 of 180 units are classified as occupied.

It does **not** tell us that the property's economic vacancy is 5%.

Those concepts are related, but they are not interchangeable.

A physically occupied unit may still experience collection loss or a concession. A non-revenue unit may have a different economic treatment from an ordinary vacant unit. A vacant unit may already be leased for a future commencement date.

The analyst should therefore understand how the rent roll classifies each unit before translating unit status into an economic assumption.

> **A red flag is a question, not automatically an adjustment.**

Seven vacant units create questions.

They do not automatically create an underwriting conclusion.

## Read the rows. Then step back.

A rent roll has two analytical dimensions:

**the unit-level detail** and **the property-level pattern**.

Consider three Harbor View 1BR leases.

Unit 102 pays **$1,245** against an illustrative market rent of **$1,390**.

Unit 219 recently leased at **$1,390**.

Unit 304 recently leased at **$1,395**.

Looking only at Unit 304, an analyst might be tempted to conclude that Harbor View's 1BR market rent should now be at least $1,395.

That conclusion would outrun the evidence.

The unit could differ in floor, view, condition, renovation level, location within the property or another characteristic not apparent from a summary rent roll.

The better question is:

**What do comparable recent leases and renewals show collectively?**

> **One strong lease is evidence. A pattern of comparable leases is support.**

That distinction matters when moving from observed contractual rents toward a supportable view of market rent.

Freddie Mac's February 2026 appraisal checklist provides an institutional example of this principle: concluded market rents by unit type should be reasonably supported by recent leasing at the subject property, including leases commencing within 90 days of the effective date of value.

## Contract rent is not market rent

Harbor View's occupied units produce **$251,910 of monthly contractual rent**.

Using the fictional illustrative market rents in our case:

The occupied contract-to-market rent difference is:

**$262,850 − $251,910 = $10,940 per month**

or approximately **4.16% of occupied illustrative market rent**.

For this case, we will call that the **occupied contract-to-market rent difference**.

The terminology is deliberate.

Analysts may encounter the term *loss-to-lease*, but definitions and implementation can vary. More importantly, the $10,940 difference does not mean Harbor View has $10,940 of immediately available monthly revenue.

Some below-market leases may have months remaining. Residents may not accept the full increase at renewal. Market-rent assumptions may require additional support. Concessions may be necessary. Units may not be directly comparable. Turnover can create vacancy and make-ready costs.

The spreadsheet identifies a difference.

Underwriting asks whether—and when—that difference can realistically be captured.

Those averages describe the existing lease population.

They do not necessarily describe today's leasing environment.

Current Fannie Mae guidance provides a useful institutional example of how rent trends are tested. Its lease-audit requirements call for reconciliation of the rent roll with signed leases and require new and recently renewed leases to be included when verifying rent trends. For properties with 101–300 units, the current minimum review is 10% of all leases.

Harbor View's fictional rent roll contains 171 occupied leases, and our educational review sample contains 18 leases.

That sample is consistent with the scale of Fannie Mae's current 10%-of-all-leases requirement for a property in Harbor View's unit-count range, but this fictional exercise is **not presented as an actual Fannie Mae lease audit**.

## Stronger face rents can hide another question

Four recent leases in our sample deserve another look:

The face rents look strong.

The concessions are part of the same leasing story.

Unit 609, for example, signed at the stated $1,760 illustrative market rent while also receiving a $750 concession.

That does not make the $1,760 contract rent incorrect.

It changes the analytical question:

**What economics were required to achieve that face rent?**

> **Higher face rent is not automatically the same thing as higher effective economics.**

That question matters at Harbor View because the T-12 showed concessions increasing late in the period—from approximately $2,000 per month earlier in the year to $7,000 in September.

The four sampled leases do not prove why T-12 concessions increased.

They provide evidence consistent with a question already raised by the operating history.

## Lease expirations turn today's rent roll into tomorrow's question

A rent roll is a snapshot.

The leases behind it continue moving.

The largest concentration occurs between April and June 2027.

Those 55 leases represent approximately **32.2% of occupied leases** and **32.6% of occupied contractual rent**.

Is that negative?

We don't know yet.

A lease expiration is not a move-out.

Some residents may renew. Some may renew at higher rents. Others may require concessions. Some may leave. New tenants may replace them quickly—or slowly. Market conditions during the rollover period will matter.

> **Expiration concentration is exposure, not a forecast of vacancy.**

The schedule tells the analyst where future leasing activity is concentrated.

The next questions are about renewal history, current renewal offers, seasonality, turnover, concessions and market demand.

## Don't make the rent roll work alone

The rent roll tells us what the lease structure looks like now.

It does not tell us everything that happened during the last twelve months.

For that, Harbor View's operating statement matters.

Likewise, the T-12 cannot show the analyst every current lease, expiration date or unit-level rent.

This is why the two documents should be read together.

Our Harbor View T-12 analysis established the property's recent rental history:

<FinancialTable tableId="harbor-view-rent-roll-t12-reconciliation" />

There is a modest upward progression.

But this is a **directional comparison, not a like-for-like revenue bridge**.

The T-12 figures describe historical operating results. The rent-roll calculation describes current occupied contractual rent at a particular date. Annualizing that point-in-time number ignores future expirations, renewals, vacancy, collections, concessions and turnover.

So we should not conclude:

**$3.023 million is Harbor View's underwritten rental income.**

Instead, we can conclude something narrower:

The current lease structure is broadly consistent with the modest recent improvement already visible in Harbor View's net rental income.

That tells us where to continue the analysis.

> **The T-12 tells us what the property reported over time. The rent roll tells us what the lease structure looks like now. Neither document replaces the other.**

And reconciliation does not mean forcing the documents to agree.

> **A difference is something to reconcile, not something to erase.**

## CRE Platform Framework: Occupancy → Rent → Lease → Concentration → Reconciliation

<PractitionerNote>This five-part sequence is a CRE Platform analytical framework, not a Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, OCC, or universal lender-prescribed methodology.</PractitionerNote>

When reviewing a multifamily rent roll, we can organize the investigation around five questions.

### Occupancy

**Who is actually occupying the property?**

Harbor View has 171 occupied units, seven vacant units and two non-revenue units.

The next questions concern vacancy duration, pre-leasing and the reason for each non-revenue classification.

### Rent

**What contractual rent is actually in place?**

Occupied contractual rent totals $251,910 per month.

Some older leases sit materially below the property's illustrative market rents. That creates potential opportunity—but not automatic revenue.

### Lease

**What does recent leasing tell us?**

Recent leases and renewals generally support stronger face rents.

Several recent new leases also contain concessions.

Both observations matter.

### Concentration

**Where is the exposure clustered?**

April through June 2027 contains 32.2% of occupied lease expirations and 32.6% of occupied contractual rent.

The concentration warrants attention without predicting the outcome.

### Reconciliation

**Does the current lease structure make sense alongside historical property performance?**

Broadly, yes.

The current contractual-rent picture is consistent with the modest recent improvement visible in Harbor View's NRI.

But “consistent” is not the same as “underwritten.”

That requires the next layer of evidence.

## What the rent roll still cannot answer

A clean rent roll can tell us a great deal about the property's current lease structure.

It cannot, by itself, prove that stated market rents are supportable.

It cannot establish how many residents will renew.

It cannot tell us whether today's concessions will persist.

It cannot establish future collection losses.

It cannot tell us how quickly a vacant unit will lease.

It cannot prove that a below-market lease represents immediately recoverable upside.

And it cannot turn current contractual rent into underwritten revenue simply because the spreadsheet can annualize it.

Those conclusions require additional evidence: signed leases, leasing activity, collection information, historical operating statements, budgets, property inspections, market data and other transaction-specific support.

Fannie Mae's current guidance demonstrates the importance of reconciling the rent roll to underlying lease evidence rather than accepting the schedule in isolation. Freddie Mac's current appraisal checklist likewise uses recent subject leasing as evidence supporting market-rent conclusions. The OCC's CRE lending handbook remains a broader institutional reference for prudent analysis of income-producing real estate rather than a substitute for transaction-level judgment.

At Harbor View, we now know more than we did from the T-12 alone.

Recent contractual rents appear stronger. Concessions deserve attention. Some older leases remain below stated market rents. A meaningful group of leases expires in the second quarter of 2027. And the current lease structure broadly supports the modest rental improvement visible in recent operating results.

We still don't know what rental revenue should ultimately be underwritten.

**That's exactly where the next analysis begins.**
