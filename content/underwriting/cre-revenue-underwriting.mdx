Harbor View Apartments appears to have four reasonable answers to the same revenue question.

Its trailing twelve-month net rental income is **$2.988 million**.

Its recent three-month performance annualizes to approximately **$3.012 million**.

Its current occupied contractual rent annualizes mechanically to approximately **$3.023 million**.

And its underwriting concludes at **$3.013 million of net rental income**.

None of those numbers is necessarily wrong.

They answer different questions.

Historical performance tells us what happened. Recent performance tells us what may be changing. The rent roll tells us what is contractually in place today.

Underwriting has to decide what the evidence supports going forward.

> **A spreadsheet can calculate revenue. An underwriter has to decide which revenue deserves to survive the underwriting.**

That does not mean choosing the highest number that can be defended.

It does not mean automatically choosing the lowest number either.

It means understanding what each number represents, reconciling the evidence behind it, and reaching a conclusion that can be explained.

## Start with what the property reported

Harbor View is a fictional 180-unit multifamily property.

Its trailing twelve-month operating statement reports:

These figures establish the historical revenue base.

They do not establish the underwriting conclusion.

The first question is therefore not:

**“What number should I replace $3.180 million with?”**

It is:

**“What does the historical result tell me, and what has changed since the period that produced it?”**

That distinction matters throughout underwriting.

A historical number can be accurate and still not be representative of the underwriting period.

A recent number can be more current and still not be sustainable.

And a projected number can be reasonable without having appeared exactly that way in the property's historical statements.

The job is to understand why.

## Revenue can be lost in different ways

Harbor View's operating statement separately identifies physical vacancy, concessions, and bad debt or collection loss.

Those are not interchangeable concepts.

A vacant unit is not currently occupied by a resident.

An occupied unit may have a concession that reduces its effective economics.

Another occupied resident may owe contractual rent that the property ultimately does not collect.

All three can affect rental revenue.

But they describe different operating conditions.

This is why a statement such as:

> “The property is 95% occupied, so revenue loss should be 5%.”

can be misleading.

Physical occupancy alone does not tell us how much contractual revenue is actually realized.

Current Fannie Mae multifamily methodology provides a useful institutional example because it separately analyzes physical vacancy, concessions, and bad debt in determining Net Rental Income.

The broader analytical principle is straightforward:

> **Physical occupancy tells you whether space is occupied. It does not tell you whether every dollar of contractual revenue is being realized.**

That distinction becomes increasingly important once we compare Harbor View's historical statements with its current rent roll.

## The annual total is only one view of performance

Harbor View reported **$2.988 million of T-12 NRI**.

But the annual total does not tell us whether performance was consistent throughout the period.

The shorter-period analysis shows:

Recent rental performance is modestly stronger.

That is an observation.

It is not yet an adjustment.

> **The evidence supports a direction before it supports a number.**

A shorter period can reveal a genuine operating change that a twelve-month total partially obscures.

<PractitionerNote>**Underwriter's Question:** Why is recent NRI stronger? If the answer is improving leases, sustained occupancy, stronger collections, or another supportable operating change, the recent performance becomes more meaningful. If the answer is one unusually favorable month, timing, or another temporary factor, it becomes less persuasive.</PractitionerNote>

That is why the underwriting question is not simply:

**“Should I use T-3 or T-12?”**

It is:

**“What caused the difference?”**

The T-3 and T-6 annualizations used here are analytical views of Harbor View's recent performance. They should not be interpreted as a universal lender methodology.

## Bring in the current rent roll

Harbor View's September 30 rent roll gives us a second type of evidence.

Of its 180 units, **171 are occupied, seven are vacant, and two are classified as non-revenue units**.

Headline physical occupancy is therefore **95%**.

The 171 occupied leases produce **$251,910 of monthly contractual rent**.

Mechanically annualized:

**$251,910 × 12 = $3,022,920.**

Now compare the available evidence:

There is a consistent directional story: recent rental performance is stronger than the full-year historical result, and the current contractual rent structure does not contradict that improvement.

But these numbers are not economically identical.

The first three are measures of historical NRI over different periods.

The last is simply current occupied contractual rent multiplied by twelve.

It does not incorporate the future effects of concessions, collection loss, turnover, vacancy, or other changes that may occur during the underwriting period.

And therefore:

> **The rent roll corroborates the direction of recent rental improvement; it does not establish underwritten NRI.**

## Market rent identifies a gap—not guaranteed revenue

Harbor View's rent roll provides another useful comparison.

Current occupied contractual rent is **$251,910 per month**.

Illustrative market rent for those occupied units is **$262,850 per month**.

The difference is **$10,940 per month**, or **$131,280** when mechanically annualized.

The spreadsheet can calculate that difference instantly.

The underwriting question is harder.

How much of it can the property actually capture?

Some older Harbor View residents are paying rents below the property's stated market levels.

The educational lease sample also shows recent leasing at stronger face rents. Older one-bedroom leases in the sample include rents around **$1,245–$1,275**, while several recent leases and renewals are around **$1,365–$1,395**. Older two-bedroom examples include rents around **$1,575–$1,625**, while recent leasing includes rents around **$1,745–$1,775**.

That is evidence that the property has achieved stronger rents on at least some recent leasing activity.

It is not proof that all existing leases can immediately move to stated market rents.

Lease expirations matter. Renewal behavior matters. Turnover matters. Concessions matter. And the market evidence supporting those rents matters.

Harbor View also has **55 occupied leases expiring between April and June 2027**, representing approximately **32.2% of occupied leases**.

That concentration creates an opportunity to reset rents.

It also creates execution exposure.

> **The spreadsheet can identify the gap. Underwriting determines whether—and when—the property can capture it.**

Market rent therefore belongs in the evidence set without automatically becoming revenue.

## Stronger face rents do not tell the whole story

Recent leasing gives Harbor View a positive signal.

But another part of the file deserves equal attention.

Concessions increased late in the T-12—from approximately **$2,000 per month** earlier in the period to **$7,000 in September**.

Our educational rent-roll sample also includes several recent leases carrying concessions.

Those observations do not prove that the sampled leases caused the historical concession trend.

They tell us that stronger face rents and concession activity are occurring in the same broader operating picture.

<PractitionerNote>**Underwriter's Question:** What do recent effective rental economics look like after concessions—not merely before them?</PractitionerNote>

That is the question a face-rent comparison cannot answer by itself.

Collection performance creates another layer.

A resident can occupy a unit and have a contractual rent obligation without every billed dollar ultimately reaching property revenue.

Harbor View can therefore support two conclusions simultaneously:

**Rental potential has improved.**

**Not all of that improvement should reach underwritten NRI.**

Those conclusions are not contradictory.

They are the reason we underwrite rather than merely annualize.

## From evidence to underwritten NRI

For the fictional Harbor View educational case, the underwriting concludes that the available evidence supports **$60,000 of gross annual rental improvement** relative to the historical revenue base.

That $60,000 is an explicit fictional underwriting judgment informed by recent NRI performance, current contractual rents, recent leasing evidence, and the direction of the property's operating trend.

It is not mechanically calculated by annualizing the rent roll.

It is not the $131,280 contract-to-market difference.

It is not a Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, OCC, or other lender-prescribed adjustment.

And the $60,000 does not all survive.

The case applies a **$35,000 underwriting offset** to the supported improvement, reflecting the judgment that concessions and collection leakage will prevent part of the apparent improvement from reaching NRI.

Importantly, this is an **incremental adjustment to the historical revenue base**.

It is not Harbor View's total concession or bad-debt assumption. Historical NRI already reflects the concessions and collection losses contained in the T-12.

Both amounts are explicit fictional underwriting judgments informed by the evidence presented in the case. Neither amount can be mechanically derived from the summarized Harbor View dataset, and neither represents a Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, OCC, or other lender-prescribed adjustment.

<FinancialTable tableId="harbor-view-revenue-rental-underwriting" />

The net improvement is **+$25,000**.

This is the underwriting judgment.

Now we test it.

Recent T-3 NRI annualizes to approximately **$3.012 million**.

Underwritten NRI is **$3.013 million**.

The difference is only about **$1,000**, or roughly **0.03%**.

That close result is useful corroboration.

It is not the derivation.

> **The adjustment is derived from the evidence; the recent run rate is then used to test whether the conclusion makes sense—not to manufacture the conclusion.**

The $60,000 improvement could become less supportable if recent leasing weakens, concessions increase, occupancy deteriorates, collections worsen, or the recent NRI trend proves temporary.

The $35,000 offset could increase if concession use or delinquency worsens.

Conversely, additional signed leases at strong effective rents, stable collections, moderating concessions, and sustained operating results could provide stronger support for the rental conclusion.

An underwriting assumption is therefore not merely a number.

It is a conclusion tied to evidence that can change.

## Other income requires a separate decision

Harbor View reported **$192,000 of other income** during the T-12.

Most months were approximately **$15,000**, while August was **$27,000**.

That makes August an obvious month to investigate. Compared with the property's typical $15,000 monthly level, the visible variance is **$12,000**.

But an unusual month is not automatically an underwriting adjustment.

> **An unusual month tells the analyst where to look. Supporting evidence determines whether an adjustment is warranted.**

<PractitionerNote>**Underwriter's Question:** What generated the additional income, and should that source reasonably continue during the underwriting period?</PractitionerNote>

The analyst still needs to understand what generated the income, whether it is part of normal property operations, whether it has occurred historically, and whether there is evidence that it should continue.

For the fictional Harbor View case, the underwriting ultimately concludes that **$15,000 of the $192,000 annual other-income total is not sufficiently supportable for the underwriting period**.

That $15,000 is an explicit **case-level underwriting judgment**. It should not be interpreted as simply removing the $12,000 August variance, nor can it be mechanically derived from the summarized monthly T-12 data presented here.

A simple $15,000 monthly run rate would produce $180,000 annually. The underwriting conclusion of $177,000 is therefore slightly below that apparent baseline.

Again, that difference is a **judgment**, not a calculation hidden elsewhere in the case data.

## CRE Platform Framework: Potential → Occupancy → Collection → Other Income → Supportability

Harbor View gives us a reusable way to organize revenue analysis.

<PractitionerNote>This is a CRE Platform analytical framework, not a Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, OCC, or universal lender-prescribed methodology.</PractitionerNote>

**Potential** asks what the property's rental structure could produce based on leases, current rents, relevant market evidence, and leasing activity.

**Occupancy** asks how much of the property is actually revenue-producing and why space or units are not occupied.

**Collection** asks how much contractual revenue survives concessions, bad debt, delinquency, and other collection issues.

**Other Income** asks what revenue exists beyond base rent, what generates it, and whether it should continue.

**Supportability** asks which revenue conclusion survives reconciliation across historical performance, current contracts, recent trends, and supporting evidence.

In practice, that becomes five questions:

1. **What was reported?**
2. **What changed?**
3. **What evidence supports the change?**
4. **What leakage or limitations offset the upside?**
5. **What number survives reconciliation?**

And there is one useful bias check:

> **Would we still defend this assumption if it made the deal slightly worse rather than slightly better?**

That is not a lender test.

It is a practitioner check against allowing the desired transaction outcome to determine the analysis.

## Shortcuts that can weaken revenue underwriting

Several shortcuts are attractive precisely because they eliminate judgment.

**“Use the T-12.”** Historical performance is important. But the property's operations may have changed.

**“Use T-3 because it is more recent.”** Recency can improve relevance. It can also amplify temporary noise.

**“Annualize the rent roll.”** Contractual rent is evidence. It is not automatically realized NRI.

**“Use market rent.”** Market rent identifies potential. It does not establish the timing or probability of capture.

**“Use the budget.”** A budget may contain valuable operating information, but management expectation is not independent proof of future performance.

**“Take the lowest number because it is conservative.”** That avoids a different kind of responsibility.

> **Conservatism is not a substitute for analysis. An unsupported low number can be just as analytically weak as an unsupported high number.**

The goal is neither to improve the transaction nor to punish it.

The goal is to reach an assumption that can be explained and defended.

## Sometimes the right conclusion is “we don't know yet”

Even a good T-12 and rent roll do not answer every revenue question.

The analyst may still need signed leases, collection reports, delinquency aging, concession schedules, leasing reports, budgets, prior-year financial statements, management explanations, market data, or lender-specific methodology.

If those materials are necessary to support an adjustment and they are unavailable, the correct conclusion may simply be:

**We don't know yet.**

That is better underwriting than manufacturing certainty from incomplete evidence.

## From revenue to NOI

At the beginning of Harbor View's analysis, four numbers appeared to compete:

**$2.988 million** — historical T-12 NRI.

**$3.012 million** — recent T-3 annualized NRI.

**$3.023 million** — mechanically annualized current occupied contractual rent.

**$3.013 million** — underwritten NRI.

They are not competing anymore.

Each answers a different question.

Historical performance tells us what happened.

Recent performance tells us what may be changing.

The rent roll tells us what is contractually in place today.

Market evidence tells us what may be possible.

Underwriting determines what the evidence can support.

For Harbor View, that process produces **$3.013 million of underwritten NRI** plus **$177,000 of underwritten other income**, for **$3.190 million of underwritten EGI**.

Revenue, however, is only one side of the operating statement.

Harbor View still has to pay taxes, insurance, payroll, repairs, utilities, management costs, and other operating expenses.

Those assumptions deserve the same questions we asked about revenue:

What happened?

What changed?

What evidence supports the change?

And what amount should reasonably carry forward?

> **Revenue establishes the top of the operating statement. Expenses determine what the property must spend to produce it. NOI is where those two underwriting conclusions meet.**
