Harbor View Apartments reports $2.215 million of trailing twelve-month NOI.

The arithmetic checks out:

**$3.180 million EGI − $965,000 operating expenses = $2.215 million NOI**

But the underwriter does not simply accept the number.

The current rent roll supports more rental income than the T-12 captured. Collections and concessions weaken part of that improvement. Some other income is not sufficiently supported as recurring. Insurance and real estate taxes are running below the amounts the property is expected to carry in the underwriting case. Payroll also requires normalization.

After working through those items, the underwriter arrives at **$2.140 million**.

The property did not suddenly lose $75,000. The analyst answered a different question.

The T-12 shows what was reported during the trailing period. Underwriting asks what operating performance is supportable for the decision being made today.

**That gap—from reported NOI to supportable NOI—is what this guide is about.**

<Warning>Harbor View Apartments is a fictional educational case. Its figures and adjustments are original illustrative assumptions, not prescribed Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, OCC, or other lender adjustments.</Warning>

## What NOI actually measures

At a conceptual level, NOI represents property operating income after the operating expenses recognized by the applicable analytical framework.

<Formula formula="NOI = Effective Gross Income − Operating Expenses" variables={[{symbol: "NOI", meaning: "Net operating income for the period"}, {symbol: "Effective Gross Income", meaning: "Property revenue after applicable vacancy, collection, concession, and other income effects"}, {symbol: "Operating Expenses", meaning: "Property operating expenses recognized by the analytical framework"}]} interpretation="The arithmetic is simple; the underwriting work is deciding which income and expenses are supportable and how the applicable framework treats them." />

Two analysts can agree completely on the arithmetic and still disagree about NOI because the real work occurs one level above the formula: Which income is sustainable? Which expenses represent normal operations? Which historical items require normalization? And what does the applicable underwriting convention include?

That last question matters. NOI is not always constructed identically across every lender, program, appraisal, model, or loan document.

<InstitutionalExample sourceId="occ-cre-lending-2022">The OCC Commercial Real Estate Lending handbook treats stabilized NOI as an underwriting estimate of property income and expenses and describes replacement reserves as a deduction in determining NOI under its convention.</InstitutionalExample>

## Start with the documents, not the subtotal

A borrower-provided operating statement may give you an NOI. It does not give you the underwriting conclusion.

Start above the subtotal.

For Harbor View, the analyst has the current rent roll, T-12, prior operating history, current budget, and supporting information for material expenses.

Those documents do not need to agree perfectly. In fact, the differences are often where the analysis begins.

If the rent roll implies stronger revenue than the T-12, why?

If the budget shows lower expenses, what changed?

If physical occupancy is high but collections are weak, what is the property actually converting into revenue?

If insurance is expected to reset materially higher, does the trailing expense still represent the property's operating burden?

**Reconciliation is not about making the documents match. It is about explaining why they do not.**

## Underwrite revenue, not the headline

Harbor View's T-12 Effective Gross Income is **$3,180,000**.

Current leasing evidence in the fictional case supports **+$60,000** of rental income. But that is not the final revenue adjustment.

Collections and concessions reduce the improvement by **$35,000**. Another **$15,000** of historical other income is not sufficiently supported as recurring in the case.

<Calculation inputs={[{label: "T-12 EGI", value: "$3,180,000/year"}, {label: "Supported rental income adjustment", value: "+$60,000/year"}, {label: "Collections and concessions adjustment", value: "−$35,000/year"}, {label: "Unsupported other income", value: "−$15,000/year"}]} formula="$3,180,000 + $60,000 − $35,000 − $15,000" result="$3,190,000/year underwritten EGI" interpretation="The rent story improves by $60,000, but the total underwritten revenue story improves by only $10,000 after collection/concession and other-income adjustments." />

A rent roll can show one story while collections tell another. The analyst has to trace the improvement through the revenue stack rather than underwriting the most attractive headline number.

<PractitionerNote>A higher rent roll is evidence—not the conclusion. Trace the improvement through occupancy, concessions, collections, and other economic losses before deciding how much belongs in underwriting.</PractitionerNote>

Fannie Mae's applicable multifamily methodology illustrates why other income deserves scrutiny rather than automatic inclusion: qualifying other income generated through ongoing operations is subject to conditions including stability, market commonality, historical support, and exclusion of extraordinary nonrecurring items.

## Historical expenses are not automatically normalized expenses

Harbor View reports **$965,000** of T-12 operating expenses.

The fictional underwriting review identifies four adjustments:

- Insurance: **+$45,000 expense**
- Real estate taxes: **+$30,000 expense**
- Payroll: **+$20,000 expense**
- Repairs and maintenance: **−$10,000 expense**

<Calculation inputs={[{label: "T-12 operating expenses", value: "$965,000/year"}, {label: "Insurance normalization", value: "+$45,000/year"}, {label: "Real estate tax normalization", value: "+$30,000/year"}, {label: "Payroll normalization", value: "+$20,000/year"}, {label: "Repairs normalization", value: "−$10,000/year"}]} formula="$965,000 + $45,000 + $30,000 + $20,000 − $10,000" result="$1,050,000/year underwritten operating expenses" interpretation="Three adjustments increase expenses and one decreases them. The direction of an adjustment should follow the evidence, not a desired NOI outcome." />

Notice the direction of the adjustments.

Three increase expenses. One decreases them.

That is intentional.

Normalization should not mean "make the underwriting more conservative," and it certainly should not mean "make the deal work."

It should mean replacing a historical amount when credible evidence indicates that another amount better represents the operating framework being underwritten.

A low historical insurance expense may deserve an upward adjustment when current support indicates a higher recurring cost. An unusual repair expense may warrant different treatment if its nature, evidence, and applicable methodology support that conclusion.

Neither adjustment should be made simply because of its effect on NOI.

## Historical, budgeted, and underwritten NOI answer different questions

<FinancialTable tableId="harbor-view-performance-comparison" />

The budget produces the highest NOI.

That does not make the budget wrong.

Management may have legitimate reasons to expect better performance. The underwriting question is whether those expectations have enough support to carry into the credit analysis.

Likewise, the T-12 is not automatically the "safe" answer simply because it is historical. Historical results can contain timing effects, temporary conditions, and unusual items.

Think of the three columns differently:

**Historical:** What happened?

**Budget:** What does management expect?

**Underwritten:** What can the analyst support under the applicable methodology?

<PractitionerNote>Underwritten does not automatically mean lowest. The objective is a supportable conclusion, not mechanical conservatism.</PractitionerNote>

## The Three-Question Adjustment Test

Whenever an analyst changes a historical number, three questions should be asked.

### 1. What caused the historical number?

Do not adjust a number before understanding it.

Was an expense unusually high because of a one-time event? Or unusually low because maintenance was deferred? Did revenue improve because rents increased, or because the statement contains a nonrecurring item?

### 2. What evidence supports a different number?

The stronger the adjustment, the stronger its support should be.

Relevant evidence may include leases, the rent roll, historical operating statements, invoices, contracts, tax information, insurance documentation, market evidence, or other credible property information.

### 3. Does the adjustment improve representativeness—or merely improve the deal?

This is the discipline check.

An adjustment should not exist simply because it increases NOI, value, or loan proceeds. Likewise, conservatism by itself is not a substitute for analysis.

**Cause → Evidence → Representativeness**

<PractitionerNote>The Three-Question Adjustment Test is a CRE Knowledge Platform educational framework, not a Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, OCC, or other institutional requirement. An adjustment that cannot survive these three questions probably needs more work.</PractitionerNote>

## Why a $75,000 NOI adjustment can matter

Assume, purely for education, that Harbor View is analyzed at a **6.50% capitalization rate**.

The 6.50% capitalization rate is an educational assumption, not a market conclusion." />

<Calculation inputs={[{label: "Underwritten NOI", value: "$2,140,000/year"}, {label: "Illustrative capitalization rate", value: "6.50%"}]} formula="$2,140,000 ÷ 6.50%" result="Approximately $32.92 million" interpretation="Illustrative value indication using underwritten NOI under the same assumed capitalization rate." />

The underwriting adjustment was **$75,000**.

The indicated valuation difference is approximately **$1.15 million**.

That is why what appears to be a modest income or expense adjustment can matter materially to a CRE transaction.

## NOI underwriting red flags

Slow down when:

- revenue rises sharply without corresponding leasing evidence;
- occupancy and collected revenue tell different stories;
- concessions disappear from the budget without explanation;
- other income increases materially without identifiable recurring sources;
- taxes or insurance are materially below current support;
- payroll appears unusually low for normal operations;
- repairs fall sharply without evidence that the historical period was abnormal;
- management expenses are absent or unusually low;
- a budget assumes simultaneous revenue growth and expense compression without adequate support; or
- NOI improves significantly while the underlying operating evidence does not.

A red flag is not automatically an adjustment. It is a reason to investigate.

## The analyst's NOI checklist

Before accepting an underwritten NOI, ask:

- **Revenue:** Can I reconcile the major revenue sources to current and historical operating evidence?
- **Vacancy and collections:** Am I looking only at physical occupancy, or also at economic losses?
- **Other income:** Is it recurring, property-related, and supportable under the applicable methodology?
- **Expenses:** Have material expense lines been analyzed individually?
- **Taxes and insurance:** Does the historical period represent the supportable underwriting run rate?
- **Normalization:** Can I explain every meaningful adjustment?
- **Evidence:** Could another analyst reproduce the reasoning from the file?
- **Convention:** Do I know exactly what is included in NOI and what sits below it?
- **Consistency:** Am I applying the same analytical standard to adjustments that increase NOI and those that decrease it?
- **Decision impact:** Do I understand what the resulting NOI does to value, debt yield, DSCR, and potential loan sizing?

If one of those answers is unclear, the NOI analysis probably is not finished.

## The underwriting lesson

Harbor View began with **$2.215 million** of reported NOI.

Nothing was wrong with the subtraction.

The underwriting work happened before the subtraction was accepted.

Revenue had to be reconciled. Collections mattered. Other income needed support. Insurance, taxes, and payroll required another look. One repair assumption moved in the opposite direction.

The resulting **$2.140 million** was not selected because it was lower.

Within our fictional case, it was selected because it was the number the analyst could better support.

**That is the distinction to carry into the next underwriting file: do not ask only whether NOI calculates. Ask whether NOI holds up.**
