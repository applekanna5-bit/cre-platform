Gross potential income and effective gross income describe different stages of a property's revenue analysis. Before comparing them, state the potential-rent basis, the treatment of other operating income, and the losses recognized between the two measures. A rental-only total is not automatically total potential property income.

This article distinguishes GPI/PGI, rental-only gross potential rent (GPR), effective rental income and EGI. Its fictional Cedar Court example starts with a uniform rental schedule and adds other income already net of collection losses. It does not calculate a complete PGI/GPI subtotal or estimate missing potential other income. The published revenue-underwriting guide addresses the next question: whether the evidence supports a forward revenue conclusion.

Cedar Court is a fictional example separate from Harbor View and the homepage model. Subtracting vacancy from the wrong starting point can understate revenue just as surely as ignoring it can overstate revenue.

## Define potential and effective income

Potential gross income (PGI) and the label gross potential income (GPI) require a stated definition rather than an assumption that they mean rental-only GPR. In the California Board of Equalization appraisal-training framework, PGI includes potential rent and other normal property income on its stated market basis. If a worksheet uses GPI, confirm whether it uses that same total and rent basis; lender and accounting definitions may differ. Cedar Court adds $60,000 of other income already net of collection losses. It does not separately calculate potential other income or a PGI/GPI subtotal.

**Gross potential rent (GPR)** is the rental-only starting amount used in this example. Cedar Court assumes the same scheduled $1,500 per unit per month for all 100 apartments, including an assumed rent for vacant units. This is a fictional uniform rent schedule, not an estimate of market rent. At full occupancy before the stated rental losses, annual GPR is $1,800,000. No separate loss-to-lease adjustment is modeled.

An actual rent schedule may use in-place rents for occupied units and an assessed rent for vacant units, or may begin with market rents. Those bases produce different starting points. Record which one you use; a market-rent total is not automatically the rent that existing leases permit the property to collect. The published contract-rent article examines that distinction in depth.

**Net rental income**, as used here, is GPR after separately defined vacancy, concessions and credit-loss deductions. **Effective gross income (EGI)** adds the stated eligible other property income to net rental income. EGI is revenue, not profit, and not necessarily cash deposited during the year. Accounting presentations may classify these lines differently, so reconcile their definitions rather than treating these labels as universal ledger accounts.

Here, **effective rental income** means the $1,683,000 annual net-rental subtotal after the separately stated rental losses. It is distinct from EGI, which includes other income, and from a per-lease effective-rent calculation. The $60,000 of other income is net of its collection losses, not net of the property's operating expenses.

Its gross-income category is broader than this article's rental-only GPR. This bridge is an explicit educational convention, not an agency underwriting worksheet.</ConventionNote>

## Build Cedar Court's annual revenue bridge

All figures are fictional USD for one twelve-month operating period. Vacancy is assumed at 5% of annual GPR, measured as a revenue deduction rather than inferred from a single-day count of vacant apartments. Concessions and credit loss are additional, non-overlapping dollar deductions on the remaining revenue exposure. These are assumptions, not recommended underwriting rates.

<Formula formula="Annual GPR = apartment count × monthly rent per apartment × 12 months" variables={[{symbol: "Apartment count", meaning: "100 units"}, {symbol: "Monthly rent", meaning: "1,500 USD per unit per month"}]} interpretation="Units and months cancel, leaving USD per year. This deliberately simplified schedule has no lease rollover or rent-regulation effects." />

1. Potential rental revenue is 100 × $1,500 × 12 = **$1,800,000/year**.
2. Vacancy removes $1,800,000 × 5% = **$90,000/year**, leaving $1,710,000.
3. Separate concessions remove **$18,000/year**. This is a full-year allowance, not a monthly concession to deduct twelve more times.
4. Separate credit loss removes **$9,000/year**, leaving **$1,683,000/year of net rental income**.
5. Add **$60,000/year of other income**, assumed recurring and already net of its own collection losses. EGI is **$1,743,000/year**.

The 6.50% rental-loss ratio is not a physical vacancy rate: it combines three revenue effects. Physical occupancy measures occupied units at a specified point or over a defined period. Other income should not conceal weak rental collections.

## Reconcile an alternative presentation

Suppose the ledger already reports $1,683,000 of net rental income and $60,000 of other income. Their sum is the same $1,743,000 of EGI. Deducting vacancy again would incorrectly reduce EGI to $1,653,000. That is a starting-basis mistake, not a different risk judgment.

Keep a gross-to-net schedule beside the ledger reconciliation. For each deduction, record its period, affected units, gross or net basis, and whether the ledger already recognizes it. Distinguish uncollected rent from rent never billed because an apartment was vacant; the same missing dollar should not be charged to both categories.

<PractitionerNote>A clean bridge explains the difference between revenue measures. It does not prove sustainability. Confirm leases, concession schedules, receivables aging, cash collections and recurring-income evidence before turning this layout into an underwriting conclusion.</PractitionerNote>

## Common mistakes

- Annualizing today's rent roll without allowing for lease expiration or concession timing. Multiplication by twelve is a unit conversion, not a forecast.
- Applying a combined economic-loss allowance and also deducting its component losses.
- Subtracting loss to lease from contractual rent that already reflects the lower lease rents. Identify the starting rent basis first.
- Including deposits or unusual fees without determining whether they are recurring property revenue.
- Comparing monthly rent with annual expenses. Use the same period throughout.
- Calling EGI profit. Expenses still have to be analyzed before calculating NOI.
